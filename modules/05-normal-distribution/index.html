Learning goals

  • Explain the shape and symmetry of a normal distribution.
  • Identify the mean as the center of a normal distribution.
  • Interpret standard deviation as distance from the mean.
  • Understand why many medical variables are approximately, but not perfectly, normal.
  • Distinguish a normal distribution from a skewed distribution.

Teaching notes

In a normal distribution, the mean, median, and mode are approximately equal. The curve is symmetric around the mean. Values close to the mean are common, while values far from the mean become progressively less common.

The standard deviation describes the typical distance of observations from the mean. Under an ideal normal model, about 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% fall within two standard deviations, and about 99.7% fall within three standard deviations.

Normality is a model, not a guarantee. Before using mean ± SD or normal-based probability statements, the distribution shape should be checked.

Visual explanation

Imagine systolic blood pressure values in a large group of adults. If the values are approximately normal, the center of the curve is close to the mean. Most people are near the mean, fewer are very low or very high, and the curve has two balanced tails.

The R activity draws this idea by showing a histogram, a smooth density curve, and vertical reference lines for the mean, mean ± 1 SD, and mean ± 2 SD.

Common mistake

A common mistake is to assume that every bell-shaped dataset is perfectly normal, or that similar mean and median values automatically prove normality. A dataset can look roughly symmetric while still having heavy tails, outliers, or other deviations from the normal model.
